Nobody Has to Conspire

Essays · 2026-11-17

Every so often, Jensen Huang's name starts showing up in the wrong section of the internet.

It's not the earnings coverage or the keynote recaps. It's the other stuff. A rumor about his private life, written in the affair-story register the tabloids perfected long ago. A framing that treats the leather jacket as a costume and the man inside it as a character. Then the memes arrive, and the rumor stops being a claim and becomes a running joke. That makes it stickier, because nobody has to believe a joke for it to stick.

I'm not interested here in whether any of it is true. I don't know, and the pattern I want to look at doesn't depend on the answer. What interests me is the shape. The stories don't trickle in. They arrive in waves, they rhyme with each other, and they tend to show up when the company looks unassailable. Watch it long enough and it starts to feel conducted.

So the natural question is: who is conducting this?

My answer is nobody. I think that answer is more boring than the alternative, and more useful.

Start with the people writing the stories. Engagement media is paid for heat. It is not paid for accuracy or importance. It is paid for clicks, shares, time on page, and the quote-post that says "can't believe this." A rumor about the most-watched executive in the most-watched industry is heat in concentrated form. An editor who passes on it gets no credit for restraint. Someone else runs it instead. Nobody had to call the editor. The payment system already did.

Then the amplifiers. A dominant figure's reputation isn't only personal. It's priced into his company. Markets pay a premium for a firm whose leader seems singular, and anything that makes him look ordinary, messy, or mockable taxes that premium a little. Competitors gain when the shine dulls. So do people betting against the stock, people who backed a different horse, and people uneasy about a whole industry orbiting one firm. None of them has to invent anything. They only have to share what's circulating with slightly more enthusiasm than they'd share the correction.

Then the slower force: political capital. Power has a temperature. While a figure is climbing, criticizing him is expensive. Allies rush to defend him, access gets cut, and you look like you missed the story. Once he's on top, the math inverts. Now he's the incumbent and the face of a concentration people feel uneasy about. He becomes the convenient target for anyone who wants to criticize an industry without criticizing an abstraction. Yesterday's untouchable becomes today's fair target. He didn't change. His position did.

Look at each actor and you find someone behaving sensibly by their own lights. The writer wants traffic. The rival wants an edge. The commentator wants to be early to the turn. The meme account wants laughs. Nobody is following a script. But stack their choices inside the same field and you get something that looks scripted, a campaign with no coordinator.

That's what I mean by incentive capture. Each actor's judgment is quietly bent by what they're paid in, whether that's money, attention, or standing. The bending is small and local. The sum is large and looks intentional.

Now the timing, because the timing is what makes it feel like a plot.

If these stories were driven by evidence, they'd arrive when evidence arrived, scattered and irregular. Instead they cluster. I think the mechanism is the Overton window, the range of things you can say in public without paying for it.

What moves isn't what's known. It's what's cheap to say. Rumors about powerful people can hum along unprinted for a long time, because printing them is expensive. There are lawyers, lost access, and the cost of punching at someone everyone admires. Then something shifts. Sentiment about the industry sours, resentment toward concentrated power builds, and one outlet goes first without getting punished. The cost drops. Everyone who was holding the thought can now afford to publish it.

So hit pieces land when the window moves, not when the evidence does. Because the window moves for everyone at once, the pieces land together. From outside, a burst of stories looks like a campaign. From inside, it's a lot of people noticing on the same day that a door they'd been leaning against has swung open.

Here I have to be careful, because a frame this neat is dangerous. Once you have it, you can explain anything with it. A frame that explains everything has stopped telling you anything.

So the frame has to pass its own test.

First, base rates. Most patterns that look coordinated turn out to be emergent. That isn't a comforting belief. It follows from how coordination works. Coordination is expensive. It needs communication, and communication leaves traces. It needs people to stay quiet, and people are bad at staying quiet, especially when the story of the conspiracy is worth more than the conspiracy. Genuinely coordinated campaigns exist, and some have been exposed in detail. But they're rarer than they feel, and they tend to leak.

Second, a disproof condition, stated plainly. The emergent read would be wrong if you found evidence of shared direction. That could be money moving from an interested party to the people producing the stories, or instructions passing between them. It could also be timing the incentives can't explain, such as stories breaking in lockstep before any shift in the window could account for it. Find that and I'd flip the read without hesitation. The point isn't to defend the frame. The point is to know what would break it.

Without that evidence, the emergent read wins on parsimony. It needs fewer moving parts and doesn't require anyone to keep a secret. It explains the clustering, the tone, the targets, and the timing with forces we can already see operating everywhere else. The conspiracy read needs all of that plus a hidden hand. When you can't see the hand and the pattern is explained without it, adding one isn't insight. It's decoration.

I build enterprise data systems, and this shape is familiar from that work. When a system produces a strange output, the instinct is to hunt for the culprit, the one bad line. Often there isn't one. Every component is doing what it was told. The strange output comes from correct parts interacting under conditions nobody designed for. You don't fix that by finding a villain. You fix it by reading the incentives the system actually runs on.

Which brings me to the practical turn.

When a narrative starts moving against a person, a company, or an industry, people reach for "who is behind this?" It feels like the serious question, but it's usually the wrong one. It sends you hunting for a conductor. When you don't find one, you either invent one or give up.

The better questions are smaller and answerable. What does each visible actor gain from this story? And what shifted to make it cheap to say now?

Answer those, actor by actor, and you've mapped the field. Most of the time the pattern explains itself. Nobody had to conspire. Everyone just had to be paid.
