The Doer Is a Story the System Tells

The strategy that won had two rules. Cooperate on the first move. After that, do whatever the other player did last time.

That was tit-for-tat, and it won Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments of the iterated prisoner's dilemma. Researchers submitted programs. Some were elaborate. They modeled opponents, probed for weakness, and defected when the math said they could get away with it. The two-line program outlasted them.

It never beat a single opponent head to head. Within any one pairing it could at best tie, because it only ever answered what it was given. It won on total score, across everyone, over time. The clever strategies kept winning small exchanges and losing the relationships those exchanges lived inside.

Here is the part I keep returning to. Tit-for-tat has no intentions. It has no virtue, no theory of the good, no self. It is a lookup rule. Yet when you watch the tournament, you see something that looks exactly like cooperation emerging, stabilizing, and spreading.

Axelrod's explanation was structural. Two things did the work: the payoff structure of the game, and what he called the shadow of the future, the expectation that you will meet this player again. When the next round matters enough, defection stops paying. Cooperation is the cheapest path through the field.

Nobody in that system is good. The structure is.

There is an older text that would not be surprised by any of this.

In the third chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna that all actions are carried out by the gunas, the qualities of prakriti, the material nature moving through a person. The self that is confused by ego looks at those actions and concludes that it is the one doing them.

That is a strong claim, and it is easy to soften it into a platitude about humility. Don't soften it. The verse does not say you should feel less important. It makes a claim about where causation lives. Action happens through you. The sense that you authored it is something added afterward, a label stuck on a process that was already running.

The Ashtavakra Gita presses harder. Its picture is of a witness. Awareness watches action move through the body and mind the way weather moves across a landscape. The hill does not make the rain. The hill is where the rain falls, and the shape of the hill decides where the water runs.

Put this beside Axelrod and the two meet. Tit-for-tat is a strategy with no doer, and it produces behavior we would happily call trustworthy. The Gita says the doer we think we have is mostly a story. One is a result from a simulation. The other is a claim about consciousness. Both say that behavior comes from qualities interacting inside a field, and the agent is where we notice it, not where it starts.

This is where it stops being philosophy and becomes a working problem.

I build enterprise data systems. Most of the failures I have seen up close were not failures of intelligence or character. They were failures of attribution. Something happened, and someone said "I decided," or "they decided," and the explanation stopped there.

Incentive capture is the plainest case. A team optimizes the metric it is measured on and slowly drifts from the goal the metric was meant to track. Ask the people involved and they will give you reasons: tradeoffs, priorities, judgment calls. The reasons are sincere. They are also mostly beside the point. Put a different set of people in the same field and you would get roughly the same drift, with a different set of sincere reasons.

That is what I mean by an authorship-attribution failure. The narrative feels like an explanation. It has a subject, a verb, and a motive. In practice it is a credit claim filed after the fact, over an outcome the incentive field already produced.

Firms do this at scale. Their strategy documents describe choices, and the choices are usually the shape of the payoff matrix, written in the first person. Language models now do it too. Ask one why it gave an answer and it will produce a fluent account of its reasoning. That account is a second output, generated after the first one. It is not a window into how the first one was made.

The cost is not philosophical. It is operational. If you believe the narrator authored the outcome, you fix the narrator. You punish the defector, praise the cooperator, replace the leader, retrain the person, tune the model's stated values. Then you put the new narrator back into the same field and watch the same outcome come back with a fresh explanation attached.

Every fix aimed at the doer misses when the doer is mostly a story.

This could easily slide into fatalism. Nobody authors anything, the field does it all, so shrug. That reading is wrong, and Axelrod is the one who shows why.

His work did not stop at describing how cooperation emerges. It showed that the field can be designed. If you lengthen the shadow of the future, so that players expect to meet again and the meetings matter, cooperation becomes more stable. If you make defection visible, so it cannot hide inside noise, retaliation becomes accurate and therefore rare. If you protect the first cooperative move, so that being nice at the opening is not fatal, reciprocity gets a foothold it can grow from.

None of these levers changes anyone's heart. The players stay exactly who they were. The emergent outcome changes anyway.

This is the turn the Gita also makes, and people who quote it as passivity tend to skip it. Krishna does not tell Arjuna to leave the field because the gunas are doing the acting. He tells him to act, fully and without clinging to ownership of the results. The teaching is not "you are not responsible." It is closer to this: you are mistaken about what you are responsible for.

Authorship may be a story. Responsibility for the field is real. It belongs to whoever can see the field, because seeing it is the precondition for changing it. Once you understand that a review process rewards hiding defects, that a metric pays for the wrong thing, or that a system's horizon is too short for anyone to expect consequences, you are no longer a narrator inside the game. You are holding one of the levers. Not pulling it is also a design choice.

So the discipline I try to practice is double vision.

The first eye acts. Fully, with care, without hedging, because the hill still has to be a hill for the water to run anywhere. Going limp is not wisdom.

The second eye attributes honestly. When something goes well, it asks what in the field made that the easy path. When something goes badly, it looks past the person who did it to the structure that made it cheap. It distrusts every sentence that starts with "I chose," including its own.

Either eye alone is a failure. Action without honest attribution builds a career out of taking credit for weather. Attribution without action turns into a very articulate kind of drift.

Held together, they give you something the clever strategies never had: an accurate picture of where outcomes come from, and the willingness to work there.
