Collecting

Collecting is a system before it is a pile. These are the starter playbooks I actually run — for comics, for pop-culture memorabilia, and for almost anything else worth keeping.

Start collecting anything

  • Define the shelf first. Name the scope before the first purchase — a theme, an era, a creator, a format. A collection with no boundary is just buying.
  • Anchors first. Pick the few pieces that define the shelf and build around them. Depth on what you love beats a shallow spread of everything.
  • Complete runs over scattered singles. A finished set is the unit of respect — one complete run is worth more than ten first issues.
  • Set a cap and keep it. A spending ceiling per batch, decided before the hunt. The cap is what keeps the hobby a hobby.
  • Keep a manifest. Every piece logged — what it is, where it came from, what it cost, what condition. Provenance is half the value, and all of the memory.
  • No filler. If a piece doesn't fit the shelf's taste, it doesn't enter — even on sale. Especially on sale.

Comics & manga

My own library is organized by signature shelves — shonen, seinen, cyberpunk, noir ink, literary graphic novels, the Golden and Silver Age Western books — with a starter canon (Dragon Ball Z, Death Note, Attack on Titan, Fullmetal Alchemist, Berserk, Vinland Saga) and complete runs as the goal. The lanes, in the order I use them:

  • The library first. A public-library card opens Comics Plus — thousands of comics and manga, free, no waitlists. Read widely there before buying anything.
  • Subscriptions for the big vaults. Shonen Jump and VIZ Manga together cost less than one volume a month and cover most of the manga canon.
  • Owned files from bundles. Humble Bundle's comics sales are the best legal route to DRM-free files you actually own — Image, Dark Horse, Kodansha, Fantagraphics. Buy complete runs when they appear; they don't stay up long.
  • Public-domain archives for the Golden Age. Comic Book Plus and the Digital Comic Museum host the pre-code classics free — with the standing caveat that public-domain status is per title, so verify before you shelve.
  • Serve it yourself. A library you own deserves a real shelf: mine is catalogued in a manifest and read through Komga, a self-hosted comics server.

Pop-culture memorabilia

The same system, applied to objects:

  • One shelf, one story. Pick the corner of pop culture that is actually yours — a franchise, a studio, an era — and let everything else go.
  • Originals over reproductions. A piece with provenance — a first pressing, a first edition, a production-era item — holds its meaning and its value. Reissues fill space.
  • Condition is the price. Learn the grading language of your category before spending; the same item is three different prices in three conditions.
  • Buy the best copy you can afford, once. Upgrading the same piece three times costs more than the good copy did.
  • Log it like the comics. Source, price, condition, date — the manifest is what turns a shelf into a collection.
